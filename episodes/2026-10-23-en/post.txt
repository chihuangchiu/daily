Title: 121 Years Ago Today, She Swam Faster Than Every Man | Gertrude Ederle

On October 23, 1905, Gertrude Ederle was born in New York City. Many people believed no woman could swim the English Channel. She did it, and she beat every man who had tried before her.

Her father ran a butcher shop in Manhattan, and she learned to swim in New Jersey, at a beach where her family spent its summers. A childhood illness left her with weak hearing, but in the water she felt completely at home. As a teenager she joined the Women's Swimming Association and began to set one record after another.

In 1924 she swam for the United States at the Olympic Games in Paris, winning a gold medal in the relay and two bronze medals in her own races. But she was dreaming of something much bigger: the English Channel, between France and England.

In 1925 she made her first attempt. Halfway across she was pulled out of the water and disqualified on a technicality. Many people said that was the end of it. She did not give up. She went home, trained harder, and came back a year later.

On the morning of August 6, 1926, she walked into the sea at Cap Gris-Nez, in France, her body coated with grease to keep out the cold. The waves were rough and the weather turned stormy, but hour after hour she kept swimming, with her support boat close beside her. After about fourteen and a half hours, she came ashore at Kingsdown, in England. She was the first woman ever to swim the English Channel, and she beat the men's record by nearly two hours.

Back in New York, huge crowds filled the streets for a ticker-tape parade in her honour, and newspapers around the world told her story. Her swim showed the world what women athletes could do. She later performed in swimming shows and even appeared in a film. Her hearing grew worse over the years until she was almost deaf, and she spent much of her life teaching deaf children to swim. She died on November 30, 2003, at the age of ninety-eight.
