Title: 394 Years Ago Today, a Cloth Seller Saw a Hidden World | Leeuwenhoek

On October 24, 1632, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was born in Delft, in the Dutch Republic. He was not a scientist. He sold cloth. Yet he was the first to see the world hidden in a drop of water.

At sixteen he became an apprentice in a cloth shop in Amsterdam. In 1654 he came home to Delft and opened a shop of his own, and he also held a post at the city hall for almost forty years.

A cloth seller needs to see the threads clearly, but the magnifying glasses of his day were not sharp enough. With no formal training in science, he taught himself to make lenses and kept his best methods a secret all his life. He made more than five hundred lenses; his best microscope magnified about 275 times.

He pointed his lens at water taken from a lake and saw countless tiny creatures swimming around. He called them "animalcules", and he described them so carefully that many can still be identified. Later he even saw bacteria. For the first time, people learned that invisible life is all around us.

In 1673 the Delft doctor Regnier de Graaf brought his observations to the Royal Society in London. He knew no Latin, so he wrote all his letters in Dutch. In 1676 he described the tiny creatures in water, and the scholars found it hard to believe, until the Society checked for itself and found that he was right. In 1680 the cloth merchant became a Fellow of the Royal Society.

He sent the Society about 190 letters. He studied red blood cells, fleas and weevils, and showed that these small insects hatch from eggs instead of appearing from dust or grain. He became famous; Peter the Great of Russia came to meet him. He kept working and writing letters until the very end, and died in Delft on August 26, 1723, at the age of ninety.

Today he is called the father of microbiology. The scientists who followed him found the germs that make us sick, and our fight against disease began with seeing this tiny life. A cloth seller's curiosity opened a whole new world.

His question for you: what around you is worth a closer look?
