Title: Jonas Salk: 112 Years Ago Today, the Man Who Beat Polio and Gave It Away

On October 28, 1914, Jonas Salk was born in New York City. He made the first successful polio vaccine, and he refused to patent it.

He was the first in his family to go to college. After medical school, he chose research over treating patients, because he wanted to study viruses. In 1947 he got his own virus research lab at the University of Pittsburgh.

Back then, polio was every parent's nightmare. Each summer, pools closed and children were kept inside. In 1952, the United States had about 58,000 cases, and more than 3,000 people died. Many children could never walk again, and some could only breathe inside an iron lung.

Salk made a vaccine from killed virus. In 1953 he even gave it to his own children. In 1954, more than a million children joined the largest vaccine trial in history. They were called the Polio Pioneers. On April 12, 1955, the verdict came: the vaccine was safe and effective.

When reporter Edward R. Murrow asked who owned the patent, Salk answered: "Well, the people, I would say. There is no patent. Could you patent the sun?" In 1963 he founded the Salk Institute in La Jolla, California. He died there on June 23, 1995, at the age of eighty.

Today, polio cases around the world are down more than 99% since 1988.
