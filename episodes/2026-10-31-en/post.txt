Title: John Keats: 231 Years Ago Today, the Poet With No Name on His Grave

On October 31, 1795, John Keats was born in London. He died at just twenty-five, with no name on his gravestone, yet he is now hailed as one of the greatest poets in the English language.

He lost his father at eight, and later his mother to tuberculosis. He was apprenticed to a surgeon and in 1815 began training at Guy's Hospital in London. In 1816 he was licensed, but he told his guardian he would be a poet, not a surgeon.

That same year, reading Homer in George Chapman's English translation inspired a dazzling sonnet. In 1818 his long poem Endymion came out, and the critics tore it to pieces. That winter, his brother Tom, whom he had nursed, died of tuberculosis too.

In the spring of 1819, living in Hampstead, he fell in love with the girl next door, Fanny Brawne. Within months he wrote odes that would never die. His friend Charles Brown said Ode to a Nightingale was written under a plum tree in the garden. Ode on a Grecian Urn ends with the words: "Beauty is truth, truth beauty."

In February 1820 he coughed up blood and told a friend: "That drop of blood is my death warrant; I must die." That September he sailed for Rome with his friend, the painter Joseph Severn. He died in Rome on February 23, 1821, at twenty-five. His gravestone bears no name, only the words: "Here lies one whose name was writ in water."

Ignored in his lifetime, he is now one of the best-loved poets in English. His house in Hampstead is today the Keats House museum.
