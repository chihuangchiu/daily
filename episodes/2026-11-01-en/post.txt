Title: Alfred Wegener: 146 Years Ago Today, the Man Who Said Continents Move

On November 1, 1880, Alfred Wegener was born in Berlin. He said the continents move, and most of the scientific world laughed. Today his idea is the starting point of plate tectonics.

In 1905 he earned a doctorate in astronomy, but weather and adventure pulled him harder. In 1906 he and his brother Kurt stayed aloft in a balloon for fifty-two and a half hours, a world record at the time. That same year he made his first trip to Greenland.

In December 1910 he wrote to his future wife: "Doesn't the east coast of South America fit exactly against the west coast of Africa, as if they had once been joined?" On both sides of the ocean he found the same fossils and the same kinds of rock.

On January 6, 1912, in Frankfurt, he told the Geological Association that the continents are not fixed: they slowly drift. In 1915 he published The Origin of Continents and Oceans. But most geologists refused to believe it. What force could push a whole continent? He had no answer, and some openly mocked him.

In 1930 he went back to Greenland a fourth time, to set up a research station in the middle of the ice sheet. He led a supply run across the ice for about forty days and reached it on October 30. On November 1, his fiftieth birthday, he left for the coast with his Greenlandic companion Rasmus Villumsen. Neither came back. Wegener's body was found in the snow on May 12, 1931.

About thirty years later, new evidence from rock magnetism and the spreading sea floor convinced scientists that continents really move. Today we call it plate tectonics, and it explains earthquakes and the rise of mountains.
