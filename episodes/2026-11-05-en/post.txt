Title: James Clerk Maxwell: 147 Years Ago Today, the Man Who Unlocked Light Died

On November 5, 1879, the physicist James Clerk Maxwell died in Cambridge, England, at only forty-eight. The signal in your phone, your radio and your TV all go back to his equations.

He was born in Edinburgh, Scotland, in 1831. As a child, whenever something moved or shone, he would ask how it worked. At fourteen, he wrote a paper on drawing oval curves with pins and string, and a professor read it to the Royal Society of Edinburgh for him, because he was considered too young.

Later, he showed that Saturn's rings are not solid but made of countless small pieces circling the planet. In 1861, using red, green and blue filters, he showed the world's first colour photograph: a tartan ribbon.

Electricity and magnetism were still two mysteries. He brought them, together with light, into one set of equations. In 1865, he calculated the speed of these electromagnetic waves, and it was almost exactly the speed of light. His bold conclusion: light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

When he died of cancer, no one had yet shown he was right. Eight years later, Heinrich Hertz created these waves with sparks in his laboratory, proving Maxwell right.

Today, radio, TV, phones and Wi-Fi rest on his equations. Einstein said: "One scientific epoch ended and another began with James Clerk Maxwell."

His question for you: when did you last ask, "How does it work?"
