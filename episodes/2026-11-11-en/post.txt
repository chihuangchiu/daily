Title: Tycho Brahe: 454 Years Ago Today, He Saw a Star That Changed the Sky

On the evening of November 11, 1572, a young Danish nobleman, Tycho Brahe, looked up and froze: a new star had appeared in Cassiopeia, brighter than Venus.

In 1566, aged twenty, he had argued with a fellow student over who was the better mathematician. They fought a duel in the dark, and part of his nose was cut off. For the rest of his life, he wore a metal nose.

People then believed the heavens never change. But Tycho measured the new star night after night and found it lay far beyond the Moon: the sky itself could change. In 1573 he published his observations in a book, De nova stella. The star stayed visible for about a year and a half.

The Danish king gave him a whole island, Hven, where he built an observatory called Uraniborg. There were no telescopes yet, so with giant instruments and his bare eyes he recorded the positions of the stars for some twenty years, with amazing precision.

Later he moved to Prague and hired a young assistant: Johannes Kepler. In 1601, Tycho died in Prague, aged 54. His records passed into Kepler's hands, and after nearly ten years of work, Kepler found that the planets move around the Sun in ellipses.

Today, that star is called Tycho's Supernova (SN 1572). The sky he charted by eye opened the door to modern astronomy.

His question for you: when everyone says no, would you trust your own eyes?
