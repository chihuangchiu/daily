Title: Banting: He Sold the Life-Saving Insulin Patent for One Dollar

On November 14, 1891, Frederick Banting was born in Alliston, Ontario, Canada. He went on to help discover insulin, and he and his colleagues sold the life-saving patent to the University of Toronto for one dollar.

He grew up on a farm, failed his first year at university, and then switched to medicine. In 1918, as an army doctor in France, he was wounded by shrapnel at Cambrai.

Back then, diabetes was almost a death sentence. Children were kept alive for months on starvation diets. On the night of October 31, 1920, unable to sleep, he jotted down an idea.

On May 17, 1921, he began experiments at the University of Toronto, in Professor J.J.R. Macleod's department, with a student named Charles Best. Working day and night, they isolated insulin. In January 1922, 14-year-old Leonard Thompson lay dying in a Toronto hospital, but after insulin injections, he lived.

In 1923, at 32, Banting shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Macleod, and he gave half of his prize money to Best. The insulin patent was sold to the University of Toronto for one dollar. Banting said: "Insulin does not belong to me, it belongs to the world."

On February 21, 1941, he died in a plane crash in Newfoundland, at just 49. His birthday, November 14, is now World Diabetes Day, and insulin has since saved countless lives.
