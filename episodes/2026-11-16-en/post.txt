Title: Achebe: The Novel That Let Africa Tell Its Own Story

On November 16, 1930, Chinua Achebe was born in Ogidi, in eastern Nigeria. He came to be called the father of modern African literature.

He grew up between Christianity and Igbo tradition. In 1948 he entered University College, Ibadan, where he read Western books about Africa in which Africans were silent extras.

He decided that Africa's stories should be told by Africans. In 1958 he published his first novel, Things Fall Apart, about an Igbo warrior whose world falls apart as colonizers and missionaries arrive. It has since been translated into more than forty languages.

He liked to quote an African proverb: until the lions have their own historians, the story of the hunt will glorify the hunter. In 1975 he challenged Joseph Conrad's classic Heart of Darkness, saying it reduced Africans to a dehumanized backdrop.

Later, a car crash left him paralyzed from the waist down, but in the United States he kept teaching and writing. In 2007 he won the Man Booker International Prize. He died in Boston on March 21, 2013, at the age of 82.

Today, students around the world still read Things Fall Apart. He let Africans tell their own story, in their own voice.

His question for you: if others keep telling your story, will you take back the pen?
