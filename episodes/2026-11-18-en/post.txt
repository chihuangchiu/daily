Title: Daguerre: The Man Who Took the First Photo of a Person

On November 18, 1787, Louis Daguerre was born in Cormeilles-en-Parisis, near Paris, France. He went on to invent the daguerreotype and is often called the father of photography.

He had little schooling, but he could draw beautifully, and as a young man he painted giant theatre scenery. In 1822, with the painter Charles Bouton, he opened the Diorama in Paris, where shifting light made huge paintings look real, and crowds lined up to see it.

He wondered: could light draw a picture all by itself? In 1829 he teamed up with the inventor Nicéphore Niépce. After Niépce died in 1833, Daguerre kept experimenting alone, until a clear image appeared on a silver-coated copper plate. In 1838 he photographed the Boulevard du Temple in Paris from his window. The exposure took minutes, so the moving carts and people vanished, but one man stood still to have his boots shined and became the earliest known person in a photograph.

In 1839 the French government bought the invention, gave him a pension for life, and shared the method with the world. Portrait studios spread fast across Europe and America, and ordinary families could keep their loved ones' faces for the first time. He died on July 10, 1851, at sixty-three.

From painted illusions to a real moment in time, he let ordinary people keep their own faces forever.
