Title: Voltaire: Jailed Twice, He Shook Europe With a Pen | 332 Years Ago Today

On November 21, 1694, the writer and thinker Voltaire was born in Paris. His real name was François-Marie Arouet.

In 1717, verses mocking the ruler of France sent him to the Bastille for almost a year. He worked on a play in his cell; when "Oedipe" opened, it was a hit, and he took a new name: Voltaire.

In 1726, a quarrel with a nobleman put him back in the Bastille, and he was then forced into exile in England. There he saw people speak freely and worship as they chose. His book praising English freedom, the "Letters on the English," was condemned and burned in Paris in 1734.

Later he settled at Ferney, near the Swiss border. In 1759 he published "Candide," a comic novel mocking blind optimism and injustice. It was banned in many places, yet it spread like wildfire across Europe.

In 1762, Jean Calas, a Protestant merchant in Toulouse, was executed, falsely accused of killing his own son. Voltaire was convinced he was innocent. He wrote letters and pamphlets and his "Treatise on Tolerance," calling for tolerance between faiths. In 1765, Calas was finally cleared.

Voltaire died in Paris in 1778, at the age of eighty-three. In 1791 his remains were moved to the Panthéon. He taught the world that freedom of thought and belief is worth defending.

His question for you: if the truth had a price, would you still tell it?
