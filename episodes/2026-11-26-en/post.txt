Title: Sojourner Truth: She Could Not Read, but Her Voice Woke a Nation | 143 Years Ago Today

On November 26, 1883, the abolitionist and women's rights activist Sojourner Truth died in Battle Creek, Michigan.

She was born into slavery in Ulster County, New York, around 1797, and named Isabella. She grew up speaking Dutch. At nine, she was sold at auction together with a flock of sheep. In 1826, when her owner broke his promise to free her, she walked away to freedom carrying her baby daughter.

Her young son Peter was illegally sold to Alabama. A mother who could not read took a white man to court, and in 1828 she won and brought her son home, one of the first Black women in America to win such a case.

In 1843, she gave herself a new name, Sojourner Truth, and began traveling to speak against slavery. In 1851, she spoke at a women's rights convention in Akron, Ohio; the speech is remembered today by the words "Ain't I a woman?"

She sold small photo cards of herself to fund her work, printed with the line "I sell the shadow to support the substance." In 1864, she met President Lincoln at the White House, and after the Civil War she helped freed people find work and land.

She never learned to read or write, yet the world still remembers her voice.

Her question for you: if the world won't let you speak, how will you be heard?
