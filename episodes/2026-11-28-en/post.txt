Title: William Blake: Called Mad in His Lifetime, Now a Giant of English Poetry | 269 Years Ago

On November 28, 1757, the poet and painter William Blake was born above his father's stocking shop in Soho, London.

As a child, he said he saw visions, including a tree filled with angels on the edge of London. In 1772, at fourteen, he was apprenticed to the engraver James Basire and spent seven years learning to cut images into copper.

In 1787 his beloved younger brother Robert died. Blake later said Robert appeared to him in a dream and revealed a new way to print: poems and pictures etched together on copper, then coloured by hand, page by page, with his wife Catherine. With this method he made Songs of Innocence in 1789 and Songs of Experience in 1794. In The Tyger he asks: "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?"

In 1809 he held a one-man exhibition at his family's old home. Almost no one came, and the only review called his paintings "the wild effusions of a distempered brain." He stayed poor all his life and died in London on August 12, 1827, reportedly singing to the end. He was buried in Bunhill Fields.

Today he is seen as one of Britain's greatest poets and artists, and his poem Jerusalem, set to music, is sung almost like a second national anthem.

His question for you: If the whole world didn't understand you, would you keep going?
