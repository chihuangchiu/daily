Title: Rosa Parks: She Stayed in Her Seat and Changed a Nation | 71 Years Ago Today

On the evening of December 1, 1955, in Montgomery, Alabama, 42-year-old seamstress Rosa Parks boarded a city bus after work and sat in the first row of the section for Black riders.

Montgomery's buses were segregated: the front seats were reserved for white riders, Black riders had to sit in the back, and when white riders had no seats, Black riders were expected to give up theirs. Parks worked as a seamstress at a department store and was the secretary of the Montgomery branch of the NAACP, where she had worked for civil rights for years.

That evening, as more white riders got on, the driver told Parks and three other Black passengers in her row to stand. The other three got up. She did not. She later wrote that she was not tired physically; she was tired of giving in. She was arrested on the spot.

As the news spread, Montgomery's Black community launched a boycott of the city buses, led by a 26-year-old pastor, Martin Luther King Jr. People walked and shared rides to work, and the boycott lasted 381 days.

In 1956, the U.S. Supreme Court upheld a ruling that segregation on Montgomery's buses was unconstitutional; that December, Black riders could finally sit at the front. Parks, however, lost her job and received constant threats. In 1957 she moved to Detroit, where she kept working for civil rights.

In 1999, Congress awarded her the Congressional Gold Medal. When she died in 2005, she became the first woman to lie in honor in the U.S. Capitol Rotunda. Today she is often called the mother of the civil rights movement.
