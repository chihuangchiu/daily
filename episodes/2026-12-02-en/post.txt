Title: Georges Seurat: He Painted a Whole Sunday With Tiny Dots | Born 167 Years Ago Today

On December 2, 1859, the French painter Georges Seurat was born in Paris.

He trained at the School of Fine Arts in Paris, but he was also fascinated by what scientists wrote about color. He noticed that two colors placed side by side, seen from a little distance, blend together in the viewer's eye. So he asked: why not let the viewer's eye do the mixing?

In 1884 he sent his first large painting, Bathers at Asnières, showing young workers resting by the Seine, to the official Salon, and the jury rejected it. He did not give up. Together with other artists he founded the Society of Independent Artists and held their own exhibition, so that rejected work could be seen too.

That same year he began painting La Grande Jatte, an island in the Seine where Parisians strolled, fished and carried parasols on Sunday afternoons. He went back again and again, making about sixty drawings and small oil studies, and spent two years finishing A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte. The canvas is about two meters high and three meters wide, and it is made entirely of tiny dots of color.

In 1886 the painting was shown at the last Impressionist exhibition and caused a sensation. A brand-new style, Pointillism, was born, and later even Van Gogh tried painting with small dots.

In March 1891 Seurat died suddenly of illness, at just thirty-one. In 1924 the Art Institute of Chicago bought the painting, and it still hangs there today. Its little dots look a lot like the pixels on our screens.
