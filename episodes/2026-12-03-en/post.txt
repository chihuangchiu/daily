Title: Ellen Swallow Richards: MIT's First Woman Graduate, Who Cleaned Our Water | 184 Years Ago

On December 3, 1842, the American chemist Ellen Swallow Richards was born on a farm in Dunstable, Massachusetts.

In those days, few girls could go to college. She taught school and worked for years to save money, and in 1868, at twenty-five, she finally entered Vassar College, where she fell in love with chemistry. She graduated in 1870.

She wanted to become a chemist, but no one would hire a woman. In 1871, MIT made an exception and admitted her as a "special student," without charging tuition. She later realized why: with no fees paid, the president could say she was not a real student. In 1873 she earned her bachelor's degree in chemistry, becoming MIT's first woman graduate and the first woman admitted to an American school of science and technology. MIT never let her earn a doctorate.

In 1876 she founded the Women's Laboratory at MIT, so that more women could learn laboratory chemistry. She did not just walk through the door; she held it open for others.

In 1887, Massachusetts launched a statewide water survey. Over two years, her team tested about 40,000 samples from rivers, lakes and wells and mapped where the water was polluted. Massachusetts then set the first water-quality standards in the United States.

She also brought science into the home, teaching people to pay attention to food, air and cleanliness, and became known as the mother of home economics. In 1908 she became the first president of the American Home Economics Association. She died in Boston on March 30, 1911, at sixty-eight.

Today, the clean water from our taps owes something to her work.
