Title: Gay-Lussac: He Rode a Balloon Alone to 7,000 Meters | Born 248 Years Ago Today

On December 6, 1778, the French chemist and physicist Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac was born in Saint-Léonard-de-Noblat, a small town in central France.

In 1797 he entered the École Polytechnique in Paris, and later worked with the chemist Claude Louis Berthollet. What he wanted most was to understand the nature of air and other gases.

In 1804, scientists wanted to know whether the air high above the Earth was the same as the air at ground level, and no one knew what would happen to a person that high up. In August, he and the physicist Jean-Baptiste Biot rose in a hydrogen balloon to about 4,000 meters. On September 16 he went up again, alone. The higher he climbed, the colder it got and the harder it was to breathe; to save weight, he reportedly even threw his chair out of the basket. He reached about 7,016 meters and collected samples of the high air. His height record stood for almost fifty years.

Back on the ground, his analysis showed that the high air was almost the same as the air below. In 1808 he published his law of combining volumes: when gases combine, their volumes form simple whole-number ratios, for example two volumes of hydrogen and one of oxygen make water vapor. This helped later scientists understand molecules. He also gave a new element its name: iodine.

He died in Paris on May 9, 1850. Today his name is one of the 72 French scientists and engineers engraved on the Eiffel Tower, and the gas laws in chemistry textbooks owe him a share.
